Three things that help your case while you wait

  • Do not sign the survey permission form. It is a negotiable legal document, not a formality. Its scope, the notice you get before crews come, what happens to your gates and fences, and who pays for damage are all terms you can change. You are not required to sign it the day it is handed to you, and not signing it immediately forfeits nothing.
  • Send us the paperwork. Photograph every page — the letter, the appraisal or its summary, the plat, the easement draft — and email it to info@nationalrow.com. A phone photo is fine. The appraisal is the document that matters most, because it is where the number came from.
  • Write down what is inside the strip. Fences, gates, cattle guards, water lines and stock tanks, pivots and irrigation, wells and tank batteries, pens, orchards, timber, trees. These are the items a first offer misses most often, and they are far easier to claim when you listed them before construction started.

Can they take it if I say no?

Ultimately, yes. A utility holding a certificate from the PUCT has the power of eminent domain, so the question is almost never whether the line gets built — it is how much you are paid for it and what the easement document lets them do afterward. That is exactly why the negotiation matters.

How much land does a 765 kV easement take?

Oncor's published specification is a 200-foot right-of-way, which works out to roughly 24.2 acres for every mile of line. But the acreage is rarely the biggest number in the file. What the corridor does to the part you keep, and what it costs to fix what the taking breaks, usually carry more value than the strip itself.
