How TxDOT gets from 17 routes to one road

TxDOT's FAQ lays out the steps. The study started with an area of about 5,000 square miles and works down in stages:

StepWidthWhere it stands
Opportunity areasBroad areasDone
Conceptual alternative routesAbout 1 mileShown August 2026 — now
Feasible routeNarrowed from the conceptsNext
Viable routesAbout 1,000 feetLater in the study
Preferred routeAbout 1,000 feetAbout 3 years out (TxDOT)
Road design and right-of-wayTypically 300–500 feetAfter the study, if funded
Only that last step decides which land is bought. Until then, the routes are lines on a planning map. They can still matter to you, for example when you sell, subdivide or build, because buyers see the same map.

If a route is chosen through your land

A new interstate on new ground is different from widening a road that is already there. Instead of a strip off the front, it can cut across a ranch or farm, split a tract into pieces, and leave parts with no road access of their own. Those effects are where most of the value in a highway case sits.

Split tracts and access

An interstate controls access. Land on the far side of a new interstate may only be reachable by a frontage road or a crossing miles away. What the pieces you keep are worth, and what it costs to make them work again, belongs in any offer.

Project influence

Texas valuation is supposed to set aside the effect of the project itself on the land TxDOT takes. Planning maps that sit on a property for years can make that harder to untangle, which is one reason to keep records of sales, listings and inquiries now.

Questions landowners between Rogers and Huntsville are asking

Tap any question to see the answer.

Has TxDOT picked a route for I-14 between Rogers and Huntsville?

No. As of October 27, 2026, TxDOT has not chosen a route. The 17 routes shown at the August 2026 open houses are conceptual alternatives, and TxDOT says all of them remain under consideration.

My land is inside one of the routes. Will it be taken?

Not necessarily, and TxDOT says it is too early to know. Each conceptual route is about a mile wide. TxDOT plans to narrow them to one feasible route, then to routes about 1,000 feet wide, then to one preferred route. A finished interstate typically needs 300 to 500 feet of right-of-way, so most land inside today's routes would not end up under the road.

When will TxDOT choose a route?

TxDOT's FAQ says a preferred route could be identified in about three years, and that the whole study will take about 8 to 10 years. TxDOT told KBTX in October 2026 that it expects the next round of public meetings in about 18 months, with narrowed or changed route options.

When would TxDOT buy land?

Not soon. TxDOT says no construction funding has been identified, no road alignment has been drawn, and no right-of-way needs have been decided. Land buying would come only after a preferred route, an environmental study, a design and funding, which is years away.

Will TxDOT use eminent domain?

TxDOT says no decisions about acquiring land have been made. If a later phase needs land, TxDOT would follow state and federal law, which includes a written offer, the Landowner's Bill of Rights and, if there is no agreement, a condemnation case.

Could I-14 just follow US 190?

It could in places. TxDOT is studying routes that use existing roads, new routes, or a mix, and it says US 190 is being considered but not every alternative follows it. The study also includes Loop I-214 around Bryan and College Station.

Can I still comment?

The comment period for the August 2026 open houses closed on October 12, 2026; TxDOT says it received more than 1,300 comments. You can still reach the study team at (979) 484-7358 or BRY_Interstate14Central@txdot.gov, and the next round of meetings is the next formal chance.

Should I do anything now?

Keep every letter from TxDOT or its consultants, note the conceptual route your land is in, and watch for the next round of meetings. If someone asks to survey your land or offers to buy it, read what you are asked to sign before you agree. It costs nothing to have us look at it.
