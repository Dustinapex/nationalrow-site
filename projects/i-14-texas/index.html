Why I-14 is being built

I-14 was created to tie Central Texas, and the large Army post at Killeen, to the Gulf Coast ports and to the interstates running east into Louisiana, Mississippi, Alabama and Georgia. TxDOT describes it as a strategic link between the military forts of the southern states and the ports used to ship equipment out. It is also meant to carry the freight and growth along the US 190 corridor, which today is a mix of four-lane highway, two-lane country road and town main streets.

Congress set the corridor in federal law in 2015 and added the North, South and spur legs in 2021. Federal designation does not build anything. It tells TxDOT that when a piece of US 190, US 83, US 87, US 69 or US 96 is rebuilt, it should be rebuilt to interstate standards: divided lanes, controlled access, frontage roads and grade-separated crossings. That is why I-14 shows up as many separate TxDOT jobs rather than one project, and why it will arrive in different places years or decades apart.

TxDOT published an I-14 System in Texas plan in 2024 after listening sessions with communities along the route. It says the exact routing in many places still has to be worked out with local governments, which is why some sections, such as Rogers to Huntsville, are still being studied.

What happens on an I-14 project, in order

Every piece of I-14 goes through the same TxDOT steps. Here is the sequence, and where your leverage sits in it.

Tap any step to see what happens.

  • First

    Schematic and environmental study

    TxDOT draws the layout, the schematic, and studies the effects. Public meetings show it to owners, and comments can still change it.

  • Then

    Public hearing and environmental decision

    A formal hearing on the proposed design, then TxDOT's environmental decision. After that, the footprint is mostly fixed.

  • Then

    Right-of-way maps and appraisal

    TxDOT surveys the exact strip it needs from each property and hires an appraiser to value it. This appraisal becomes the base of the offer, so what it leaves out is what the offer leaves out.

  • Next

    Initial offer and the Landowner's Bill of Rights

    Texas law requires a written initial offer with the appraisals TxDOT has from the last ten years, and the Landowner's Bill of Rights. TxDOT must wait at least 30 days before making a final offer.

  • Next

    Final offer

    The final offer must be at least TxDOT's own written appraisal, with the Bill of Rights sent again at least seven days before. You get at least 14 days to respond.

  • If you cannot agree

    Special commissioners

    TxDOT files a condemnation case and a judge appoints three local landowners as special commissioners. They hear both sides and set an amount.

  • Last resort

    Objection and trial

    Either side may object to the commissioners' award and take the amount to court for a judge or jury to decide.

Where the leverage actually is: most of it sits between the appraisal and the final offer. Once you sign a deed to TxDOT, the sale is done. The cheapest hour you will ever spend is the one before you sign anything.

What an I-14 taking costs a landowner

A highway taking is usually not an easement. TxDOT normally buys the land outright, and the strip becomes part of the road for good. That is very different from a pipeline or power line, where you keep the land under an easement.

The strip is rarely the whole loss

On most I-14 pieces the road is being widened along today's highway, so what TxDOT takes is frontage: the front of a ranch, a home site, a store's parking lot, a sign, a well, a fence line. The price of that strip per acre is usually the easiest number in the offer. The harder ones are what the taking does to everything you keep.

Access changes when a road becomes an interstate

An interstate controls access. Where TxDOT builds an I-14 main lane, driveways and gates stop opening straight onto it, and traffic moves to frontage roads, turnarounds and interchanges. In Texas, a loss of access is compensable only in some situations, so it has to be documented carefully, with how the property actually works before and after.

Frontage land and land near interchanges

Land along an interstate frontage road or near a new interchange can be worth more after the project, not less. TxDOT's appraisal is supposed to value the land you lose as it was before the project, without the bump the project itself creates, and the land you keep as it is after. Getting those two sides right is where offers on highway projects most often go wrong in both directions.

Where the money actually is

In almost every highway case the biggest items besides the land are damage to the remainder, the cost to cure (moving a fence, a gate, a well, a sign, a septic system or a parking layout so the property works again) and, for businesses, the effect on visibility and access. Those are the items a first offer most often leaves thin.

What the offer will look like — and what it usually leaves out

When TxDOT's offer arrives it will not say "here is our opening bid." It comes as a package: a cover letter, the appraisal or a summary of it, a right-of-way map of the part TxDOT wants, and the Landowner's Bill of Rights.

The three numbers every offer package contains

ComponentWhat it pays forTypically
Value of the part acquiredThe strip itself, priced against comparable salesLargest single line
Damages to the remainderLost value to everything you keep — shape, access, frontage, use, marketabilityFrequently understated
Cost to cureWhat it costs to fix what the taking broke — fences, gates, wells, signs, driveways, parkingFrequently omitted

How much does the back half matter? In one recent Texas highway offer package our team reviewed, damages to the remainder made up about 39% of the total offer and cost to cure another 29%. Nearly seven of every ten dollars came from something other than the raw land price. If your offer is almost all a per-acre land number with little for damages and cure, that is not because your property has none. It is because nobody quantified them.

How to read your own package in ten minutes. Find the total. Find the per-acre or per-square-foot figure and the area it is applied to. Multiply. Whatever is left over is what they are paying for damages and cure. If that leftover is small or zero, you have found your issue.

What their offer may miss on an I-14 project

TxDOT's appraiser works for TxDOT. That does not make them dishonest — it makes them the other side's expert. These are the items that most often come in low or not at all.

Tap each one to see what it means on your property.

Damage to the Remainder

A narrower lot, a house closer to the traffic, a ranch with a new corner cut off. What the land you keep is worth after the project is a separate number from the strip.

Access and Driveways

Where a gate or driveway moves to a frontage road, a turnaround or a new crossing, how the property works changes. It has to be documented before and after.

Signs, Parking and Layout

For a business, the loss of a sign, parking spaces or a truck turning area can cost far more than the land under it.

Wells, Septic and Utilities

A water well, septic field, propane tank or power service inside the strip has to be replaced or moved, and that cost belongs in the offer.

Fences, Gates and Pens

Miles of new fence, cattle guards, gates and pens near the road are a cost to cure, not a favor.

Split Tracts

Where a new road or relief route cuts across a tract, the pieces on each side can each be worth less than their share of the whole.

Development Potential

Land that could have been divided, built on or sold as frontage has a value beyond its farm or ranch use. A taking that ends that potential is compensable.

Temporary Construction Easements

Room TxDOT uses only during construction has its own value, for the time it is actually used.

Questions landowners along I-14 are asking

Tap any question to see the answer.

Is I-14 approved?

Congress made the route a future interstate in 2015 and added more legs in 2021, so the corridor is set in federal law. But there is no single approval for the whole thing. Each piece becomes interstate when TxDOT upgrades it, and every upgrade is its own project with its own environmental review and public hearing. As of October 27, 2026, only about 25 miles, Copperas Cove to Belton, are signed as I-14.

Will my land be taken for I-14?

Only if it sits where TxDOT upgrades a piece of the route. Most of I-14 follows highways that already exist, so the usual taking is a strip along your frontage for wider lanes, frontage roads or an interchange. A few places may get a road on new ground, such as a relief route around a town or the Rogers-to-Huntsville section TxDOT is still studying. Being near the line on this page does not mean you will be contacted.

When will TxDOT start buying land?

TxDOT buys land before it puts a job out to bid, usually one to three years ahead. Of the I-14 jobs on this page, the earliest bid TxDOT expects is Mar 2027. On US 83 north of Menard, TxDOT's own schedule shows land purchases from 2027 to 2029. As of October 27, 2026, TxDOT's public right-of-way parcel map shows no parcels being bought yet on the I-14 jobs listed here.

How do I find out which I-14 project my land is on?

Use the address check on this page, or look at the letter TxDOT sent you. It will name the highway and usually a job number in the form 0000-00-000, called a CSJ. Send us the letter and we will match it to the project and tell you where it stands.

Do I have to let TxDOT survey my land?

TxDOT will often ask to survey before it makes an offer. What you are asked to sign matters, so read it before you agree and ask what the crew will do, where and for how long. If you are not sure what you are being asked to sign, send it to us first. It costs nothing to check.

Do I have to accept TxDOT's offer?

No. Texas law requires TxDOT to make a written initial offer, give you the Landowner's Bill of Rights, and wait at least 30 days before a final offer, which must be at least the amount of its own written appraisal. You then have at least 14 days to respond. Those offers are TxDOT's opening position, not the last word on what your land is worth.

What happens if we cannot agree on a price?

TxDOT can file a condemnation case. A judge appoints three local landowners as special commissioners, who hold a hearing and decide the amount. Either side can object to their award and take the case to court. Most cases settle well before that point.

No. National ROW is a right-of-way and condemnation consulting firm, and we represent property owners only, never the agency. We do the valuation and negotiation work. When a case needs a lawyer, we say so and coordinate with condemnation counsel.
