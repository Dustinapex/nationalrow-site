Why the Hillje–Blu Lacy line is being built

ERCOT, the grid operator for most of Texas, concluded in its 2024 Regional Transmission Plan that the existing 345 kV network will not keep up with the load growth it now forecasts. Its answer is a statewide 765 kV backbone — the first extra-high-voltage lines of that size in ERCOT. The Permian Basin lines in West Texas were the first wave. The Eastern Backbone is the second.

The Eastern Backbone is a loop of seven new 765 kV segments, about 1,109 miles in all, submitted jointly by AEP Texas, CPS Energy, Oncor and CenterPoint: Dinosaur to Watermill, Watermill to Martin Lake, Martin Lake to Hillje, Hillje to Blu Lacy, Blu Lacy to Howard, Howard to Bell East, and Bell East back to Watermill. ERCOT’s estimate for the whole loop is about $9.38 billion. ERCOT’s Technical Advisory Committee endorsed it on November 19, 2025, and it went to the ERCOT Board in December 2025.

Hillje to Blu Lacy is the Gulf Coast leg. It ties a new 765 kV station in Wharton County to a new one in Nueces County, and Blu Lacy then becomes the starting point of the companion line north to San Antonio.

None of that decides where the line goes. ERCOT settled that a line is needed between two points; the Public Utility Commission of Texas decides which land it crosses, and only after the utilities file an application with specific routes.

What this means for you: the question of whether a line gets built was answered at ERCOT. The routing fight — which land it crosses — has not formally started, and the compensation fight starts after that. Owners who understand both early are the ones who get heard.

What a 765 kV easement costs you on Coastal Bend farm and ranch land

A 200-foot permanent easement across a mile of your property is a little over 24 acres inside the strip alone. On this project the strip is rarely the expensive part. What follows is what a 765 kV corridor actually does to a Coastal Bend tract, in the order landowners tend to discover it.

You keep the land and lose the use of it

A transmission easement is not a sale of the strip. You still own it and still pay taxes on it, but you cannot build on it, and what you can plant, store or run through it is limited by the easement language. On grazing land the strip stays usable for cattle; on irrigated or cultivated ground the towers and clearances change how the whole field is farmed.

Towers in the field

At roughly 1,200 feet between structures, a mile of line carries four to five lattice towers. Each one is a permanent obstacle that planters, sprayers and harvesters work around for the life of the line, and each corner is a weed patch someone has to manage.

Rice levees, canals and aerial application

Rice, cotton and grain sorghum in this part of Texas lean heavily on aerial application. A 765 kV line with towers up to 180 feet tall changes how — and whether — a crop duster can work a field next to it. On leveled rice ground, a tower line can cut across levees and canals that took years to set.

Hunting and wildlife lease income

Coastal ranches in Refugio, Goliad, Bee and Victoria counties often earn as much from hunting and wildlife leases as from cattle. A 200-foot cleared corridor and a line of steel towers is something a lessee notices, and lease income is a measurable loss.

Development value near Victoria and Corpus Christi

Land on the path of growth around Victoria, and on the Nueces and San Patricio side near Corpus Christi, is valued on what it can become. A corridor through the middle of a future subdivision, or across road frontage, takes value that a per-acre agricultural number will never capture.

Oil, gas and pipelines already on the land

Much of this ground already carries pipelines, flowlines, lease roads and well sites. A transmission corridor on top of existing burdens is a cumulative-burden argument — the tract is worth less with each one, and the last one in should not be priced as if it were the first.

What the offer will look like — and what it usually leaves out

Once the Commission signs an order and your tract is on the chosen route, the sequence is survey, appraisal, written offer. Under Texas Property Code Chapter 21 that first written offer has to be a bona fide offer supported by an appraisal. Bona fide is a procedural test. It does not mean the number is correct, and it does not mean the appraisal looked at everything it should have.

What just compensation is actually made of

ComponentWhat it pays forTypically
Value of the part acquiredThe strip itself, priced per acre against comparable salesLargest single line
Damages to the remainderLost value to everything you keep — severance, access, layout, irrigation, marketabilityFrequently understated
Cost to cureWhat it costs to fix what the taking broke — fences, gates, cattle guards, crossings, reseeding, drainageOften omitted entirely
Temporary construction easementExtra ground used during construction, separate from the permanent stripPriced as an afterthought

An offer that consists of one per-acre number multiplied by the acres in the strip has quietly priced three of those four lines at zero.

The appraisal that supports the offer was ordered and paid for by the party buying your land. That does not make it dishonest — it makes it one opinion of value, produced for someone whose interest is a low number. You are entitled to put a second opinion in front of them, and on Coastal Bend tracts the gap between the two is usually not small.

Before you sign anything, get these in writing

  • Exactly where the corridor crosses, and where the access roads and pulling sites will be
  • How many crossings you get, where they are, and who maintains them
  • What happens to fences, gates and cattle guards, and to what standard they are restored
  • Restoration terms for ruts, compaction, caliche, drainage and reseeding — with a time limit
  • Whether the easement language allows future lines, distribution taps, or third-party attachments on the same strip
  • How the temporary construction easement is priced and when it ends

What their offer may miss on this project

Eight things that are compensable, that Coastal Bend landowners routinely are not paid for, and that a per-acre offer does not address.

Tap each one to see what it means on your tract.

Damage to the Remainder

A 200-foot high-voltage corridor can reduce the market value of your whole tract, not just the strip. Buyers of Coastal Bend ranch and farm land price a 765 kV line in.

Rice Levees, Irrigation and Field Shape

A corridor that crosses leveled rice ground, splits a field or clips an irrigation layout costs you the productivity of the whole unit, not the acres inside the easement.

Aerial Application

Towers up to 180 feet tall change how a crop duster works your fields. Lost or more expensive aerial application is a real, recurring cost.

Access and Crossings

Where their trucks enter, how often, and what happens to your gates, ranch roads, cattle guards, fences and drainage during and after construction.

Temporary Construction Easement

Extra ground used during construction — laydown yards, pulling sites, crane pads, access roads. It is separately compensable and often under-priced or left vague.

Hunting and Wildlife Leases

Lease income lost or reduced because of the cleared corridor, the towers and years of construction traffic.

Development Potential

Frontage, subdivision potential and future building sites near Victoria, Corpus Christi and the towns along the route. A corridor across them takes highest-and-best-use value.

The Easement Language

Whether the easement allows future lines, wider clearing, distribution taps or third-party attachments on the same strip — and who restores the land, to what standard, for how long.

Has a route been chosen for the Hillje–Blu Lacy 765kV line?

No. As of November 20, 2026 no application has been filed with the Public Utility Commission of Texas. CenterPoint and AEP Texas have 100 preliminary links on their public comment map. AEP’s November 10, 2026 fact sheet targets filing in spring 2027 and a PUCT decision with a final route in early 2028.

Should I go to the December open house?

If you can, yes. The open houses are drop-in, with no formal presentation: Wednesday, December 9, 2026 at Padilla Hall on the Refugio County Fairgrounds, and Thursday, December 10 at the RMB Fairgrounds in Robstown, both 3–7 p.m. Bring an aerial of your land and mark what a line must avoid — homes, wells, pivots, airstrips, cemeteries, recorded sites. Comments made now can shape which routes get filed. You do not need to sign anything there.

A land agent wants permission to survey my property. Do I have to sign?

Read it carefully before you sign anything. A survey permission form is an agreement, and terms like advance notice, which gates and roads crews may use, who repairs damage, and liability are worth having reviewed. Send it to us and a senior consultant will tell you what it says and what is missing. We are not a law firm; when a question needs a lawyer, we coordinate licensed counsel.

How wide is the easement and how tall are the towers?

AEP’s published figures for this project are a typical right-of-way of 200 feet, steel lattice structures typically 160 to 180 feet tall, and about 1,200 feet between structures. Exact heights and widths can vary by location. On a 765kV line, 200 feet across a mile of land is a little over 24 acres.

Can landowners stop the line?

ERCOT has already concluded the line is needed between Hillje and Blu Lacy. What is still open is the route. Once the application is filed, landowners who intervene by the deadline can argue for and against specific routes at the PUCT. Whether the line is built is rarely the winnable fight; where it goes, and what you are paid if it crosses you, usually are.

What is PUCT Project No. 60061?

It is a public-input project the Public Utility Commission of Texas opened on July 30, 2026, so the public could comment before the application is filed. It is not the case that will decide the route — the application will get its own docket number when it is filed. Landowners are already filing protests and comments in Project No. 60061.

When would I actually be paid?

Not before the PUCT picks a route. AEP’s schedule has easement acquisition beginning in spring 2028. Under Texas Property Code Chapter 21 the utility must make a bona fide offer supported by an appraisal, with a final written offer at least 30 days after the initial one, and must give you the Texas Landowner’s Bill of Rights. That first number is a starting point, not a ceiling.
