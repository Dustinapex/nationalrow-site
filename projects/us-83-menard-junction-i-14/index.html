The two US 83 projects, piece by piece

TxDOT is handling US 83 as two projects, with a gap through the town of Menard. Here is where each runs, what TxDOT has published for it, and how far along it is.

Tap a project to open it.

North of Menard · CSJ 0035-05-059 · about 11 miles

From the Concho County line south to US 190 north of Menard, all in Menard County. Today it is a two-lane road with alternating passing lanes on a strip about 100 to 120 feet wide. TxDOT proposes about 470 feet. Phase I widens it to a four-lane divided highway, and Phase II adds frontage roads. The project also realigns the FM 3463 intersection, about half a mile, and improves the US 83 and US 190 intersection.

This is the furthest along: the public hearing was held September 29, 2026, Project Tracker puts its estimated cost at $84.1M with bidding expected in Jan 2028, and TxDOT's schedule shows land purchases in 2027 through 2029.

Menard to Junction · three jobs · about 26 miles

From RM 2291 south of Menard to I-10 north of Junction, through southern Menard County and Kimble County. TxDOT's 2024 fact sheet shows the same approach: four lanes first, frontage roads later, with the road strip growing from 120 to 370 feet.

Why TxDOT is widening US 83

US 83 between Eden and Junction is a two-lane road with passing lanes that connects I-10 at Junction to San Angelo, Abilene and the rest of West Central Texas. TxDOT's stated goals for the project are safety, connectivity and mobility on a road that carries growing truck and through traffic.

In 2021 Congress made this stretch of US 83 one of the spurs of Interstate 14. That is why TxDOT's plans show two phases: a four-lane divided highway now, and frontage roads later so the main lanes can become an interstate with controlled access. TxDOT is asking for the wider road strip in this project, so the land question is being settled now even though the second phase depends on future funding.

The I-14 designation does not by itself take any land. The US 83 projects do, because they need a wider road strip. That is why the timing of this project, not the interstate label, is what matters to owners along the road.

What happens next on US 83, in order

The public hearing is done. Here is what comes next for owners along the road, and where your leverage sits.

  • Done — 2023 to 2025

    Public meetings

    TxDOT held public meetings in December 2023, April 2024 and January 2025 while drawing the layout.

  • Done — September 29, 2026

    Public hearing, north project

    TxDOT presented the interim and ultimate schematics in Menard. Comments closed October 14, 2026.

  • Next

    Environmental decision and right-of-way maps

    After its environmental decision, TxDOT surveys the exact strip it needs from each property and hires appraisers to value it.

  • 2027 to 2029 (north)

    Initial offer and the Landowner's Bill of Rights

    TxDOT sends a written initial offer with the appraisals it has from the last ten years and the Landowner's Bill of Rights, then waits at least 30 days before a final offer.

  • Then

    Final offer

    The final offer must be at least TxDOT's own written appraisal, with the Bill of Rights sent again at least seven days before. You get at least 14 days to respond.

  • If you cannot agree

    Special commissioners

    TxDOT files a condemnation case and a judge appoints three local landowners as special commissioners, who hear both sides and set an amount.

  • Last resort

    Objection and trial

    Either side may object to the commissioners' award and take the amount to court.

Where the leverage actually is: most of it sits between the appraisal and the final offer. Once you sign a deed to TxDOT, the sale is done. The cheapest hour you will ever spend is the one before you sign anything.

What the US 83 widening costs a landowner

On a highway project TxDOT normally buys the land outright. The strip becomes road for good. That is very different from a pipeline or power line, where you keep the land under an easement.

A much wider road strip

TxDOT's notices describe the north project's road strip growing from about 100 to 120 feet to about 470 feet, and the south project's from 120 to 370. If the extra width came evenly from both sides, that is roughly 42 to 45 acres for every mile of road on the north project and about 30 on the south. On a ranch with a mile of frontage, that is a lot of land. On a home site or a store close to the road, it can be the front yard, the parking lot or the building itself.

Frontage, gates and the rest of the ranch

Along US 83 most of the land is ranch and hunting country. The questions that drive value are rarely the per-acre price of the strip. They are where the new fence goes, what happens to the gates, cattle guards, pens and water near the road, how hunting and recreational use of the rest of the place changes, and how a buyer would see the ranch with a divided highway and, later, frontage roads along it.

Access

A divided highway adds a median, which can change where you can turn in and out. When frontage roads come, the main lanes become controlled access. In Texas, a loss of access is compensable only in some situations, so it has to be documented carefully with how the property actually works today.

Where the money actually is

In almost every highway case the biggest items besides the land are damage to the remainder and the cost to cure: moving fences, gates, wells, pens, signs and driveways so the property works again. Those are the items a first offer most often leaves thin.

What the offer will look like — and what it usually leaves out

When TxDOT's offer arrives it will not say "here is our opening bid." It comes as a package: a cover letter, the appraisal or a summary of it, a right-of-way map of the part TxDOT wants, and the Landowner's Bill of Rights.

The three numbers every offer package contains

ComponentWhat it pays forTypically
Value of the part acquiredThe strip itself, priced per acre against comparable salesLargest single line
Damages to the remainderLost value to everything you keep — shape, access, frontage, use, marketabilityFrequently understated
Cost to cureWhat it costs to fix what the taking broke — fences, gates, cattle guards, wells, pens, drivewaysFrequently omitted

How much does the back half matter? In one recent Texas highway offer package our team reviewed, damages to the remainder made up about 39% of the total offer and cost to cure another 29%. Nearly seven of every ten dollars came from something other than the raw land price. If your offer is almost all a per-acre land number with little for damages and cure, that is not because your property has none. It is because nobody quantified them.

How to read your own package in ten minutes. Find the total. Find the per-acre figure and the acreage it is applied to. Multiply. Whatever is left over is what they are paying for damages and cure. If that leftover is small or zero, you have found your issue.

What their offer may miss on US 83

TxDOT's appraiser works for TxDOT. That does not make them dishonest — it makes them the other side's expert. These are the items that most often come in low or not at all.

Tap each one to see what it means on your tract.

Damage to the Remainder

A ranch with a wider highway along its front, a house that ends up closer to the traffic, a corner cut off by a realigned intersection. What you keep is worth something different afterward.

Fences, Gates and Cattle Guards

Miles of new right-of-way fence, new gates and cattle guards, and pens or traps near the road are a cost to cure that belongs in the offer.

Water and Wells

A well, tank, trough or water line inside the strip has to be replaced or moved so the pasture still works.

Hunting and Recreational Value

In Menard and Kimble counties many ranches sell on their hunting and recreational use. Noise, access and a wider road along the front can affect that value.

Access and Turns

A median and, later, frontage roads change how you get in and out. How the ranch works before and after has to be documented.

Homes and Improvements Near the Road

A house, barn, sign or business close to US 83 can lose its yard, parking or setback even if the building itself is not taken.

Pecans and Trees

Mature pecans and shade trees along the road are not priced like pasture.

Temporary Construction Easements

Room TxDOT uses only during construction has its own value, for the time it is actually used.

Questions landowners on US 83 are asking

Tap any question to see the answer.

Is US 83 becoming I-14?

Yes, in stages. US 83 from Eden to I-10 at Junction is one of the routes Congress added to the I-14 system in 2021. TxDOT's plan is a four-lane divided highway first, then frontage roads on both sides later to bring it to interstate standards. TxDOT says that second phase depends on funding and interstate planning.

How much of my land will TxDOT need?

It depends on where your tract sits and which side of the road TxDOT widens toward. On the north project, TxDOT's 2026 notices describe today's road strip as about 100 to 120 feet wide and the proposed one as about 470 feet. On the south project, its 2024 fact sheet shows 120 feet growing to 370 feet. If the full extra width came from both sides evenly, that is roughly 42 to 45 acres per mile on the north project and about 30 acres per mile on the south. That is our arithmetic on TxDOT's published widths; your own share will come from TxDOT's right-of-way map.

When will TxDOT contact me?

For the north project, TxDOT's project page shows land purchases in 2027 through 2029, and Project Tracker expects bidding in Jan 2028. The south project's jobs are expected to bid in Feb 2029, Mar 2030, so land buying there should follow later. As of October 27, 2026, TxDOT's public right-of-way parcel map shows no parcels being bought yet on these jobs.

Was the September hearing my last chance to comment?

The formal comment period for the September 29, 2026 hearing closed on October 14, 2026. TxDOT's environmental decision comes next. You can still contact the San Angelo District about your property at (325) 944-1501, and what you say to TxDOT's right-of-way staff later still matters.

Will my house or business be taken?

TxDOT's May 2026 notice for the north project said no homes or other buildings were expected to be displaced at that time. That can change with final design, and TxDOT has not published a building count for the south project. If a structure on your property is close to the road, have it looked at.

My land is in the town of Menard. Is it included?

The north project ends at US 190 north of Menard and the south project starts at RM 2291 south of Menard, so US 83 through town is not part of either widening as TxDOT has described them. The north project also includes about a half mile of FM 3463 and a short piece of US 190 where they meet US 83.

The road runs past my ranch gate. What happens to my access?

In the first phase, a divided highway adds a median, which can change where you can turn in and out. In the second phase, frontage roads carry local traffic and the main lanes become controlled access. How your gates, crossings and pens work before and after is part of what you should be paid for, so document it now.

No. National ROW is a right-of-way and condemnation consulting firm, and we represent property owners only, never the agency. We do the valuation and negotiation work. When a case needs a lawyer, we say so and coordinate with condemnation counsel.
